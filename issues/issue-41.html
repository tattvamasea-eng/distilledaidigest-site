The Week Agents Worked Both Sides

Always-On Agents Agent Attacks Gartner 2027 AI Costs Supply Chain
Adds a beginner or technical note under each story. New to agents? Start with Agent 101 →

This week the same technology showed up on both sides of the desk. Anthropic released an agent that watches your inbox and acts for you while you are away. A single hacker pointed a swarm of agents at more than 1,200 websites and got into over 100 companies for about the price of a dinner per break-in. A popular open-source tool that thousands of AI applications route through was poisoned for the second time this year, and it shipped malware that steals the very keys those applications run on.

Gartner gave the moment a map, putting agent identity and the agent harness among its top trends for 2027. A Splunk survey gave it a price tag: most organizations cannot control what their AI costs. The pattern is that an agent works for whoever holds its keys. This issue is about making sure that is you, and about knowing what each one costs.

Story 01

Claude Duet and the Always-On Agent: You Will Pick One

What launched: Anthropic opened a research preview of Claude Duet, a separate app in which Claude comes to you rather than waiting for a prompt. It runs on Claude Opus 5.5 on its own cloud computer, so tasks keep going after you close the app. It checks your connected accounts every few hours, sends a morning brief, and asks before it sends, pays, signs, or deletes anything. Access is by waitlist, for Pro and Max subscribers in the US only. Team and Enterprise plans are not included, and the background work counts against the plan's usage limits.

The field is now crowded: Duet arrives after Grok Bot (Issue #29), Meta's Muse (#36), OpenAI's Dots (#37), and Google's Gemini agent (#38). Each one wants the same things: your email, your calendar, your memory, and permission to act.

My read: the always-on agent market is splitting in two. Muse and Instinct are built for personal life. Dots, Duet, and Grok Bot lean toward work. Duet sits in that second group, but it is aimed at professionals and is not yet sold to enterprises. And this is not a mix-and-match market. An always-on agent is only useful if it holds your context, so running two means two sets of access to the same inbox and two agents acting on the same threads. You pick one.

Why it lands on the CIO's desk now: Duet is sold to individuals. A professional on a personal Pro or Max plan can connect it to the tools they work in before the organization has chosen any agent at all. The choice of platform can be made for you, one employee at a time.

▌ The Implication

Choose your always-on agent deliberately, before your people choose it individually. Whichever one you pick will hold the keys to your inboxes and calendars.

In simple terms

Most AI assistants wait for you to type a question. Duet works the other way: it watches your email and calendar, tells you what needs attention, and does tasks for you in the background. It asks permission before doing anything that cannot be undone, like sending a message or paying a bill.

Under the Hood

Duet runs an Opus 5.5 agent loop on a hosted machine and polls connected accounts every few hours instead of streaming them continuously, which keeps plan usage down. Consequential tool calls (send, pay, sign, delete) are gated on an explicit user approval, and background runs draw on the same quota as interactive use.

Story 02

$50 a Break-In: Ask Why, Not How

What happened: Reuters reported that a Chinese-speaking hacker used AI agents to break into the websites of more than 100 companies, including Deere, SharkNinja, and Cummins, across the US, Canada, Australia, and Japan. The research came from Snorre Fagerland of the security firm NorForge. He found the operator's control panel for Cairn, an open-source agent platform, left exposed on the internet. The agents attacked more than 1,200 sites, mostly on their own, at roughly $50 in model tokens per successful hack. The instructions were reportedly under 100 Chinese characters. Cairn's author told Reuters the tool was not built for attacks.

How is the boring part: the agents went after well-known weaknesses such as SQL injection, unsafe file uploads, and authentication bypasses. These are the flaws standard scanners have looked for over many years. Reuters could not establish how much data, if any, was taken.

My read: the real question is why. At $50 a break-in, an attacker does not need a big payoff from any one victim. Reselling access, skimming payment data, or a small extortion demand all pay for themselves many times over at that price. The economics of attack have flipped: it is now cheap to try everyone. Assume you are being tested cheaply, at volume, right now.

What changes for defenders: controls sized for a rare, expensive, targeted attacker do not fit an attacker that costs almost nothing to run. Your exposure is no longer measured by whether you are worth attacking. It is measured by whether you have one unpatched web form among thousands of probes.

▌ The Signal

When an attack costs $50, every company is worth trying. Close the known, ordinary flaws on every public site you own, because the agents will find the one you forgot.

In simple terms

One person used AI programs to try to break into more than a thousand company websites and got into over a hundred. The AI did almost all the work, and each successful break-in cost about $50. When attacks are that cheap, criminals can afford to try every company, not just big targets.

Under the Hood

Cairn takes a start state and a goal and runs agents step by step toward it. NorForge reports the agents probed for SQL injection, unsafe file uploads, insecure deserialization, authentication bypass, and server-side template injection, all classes that common scanners and web application firewall rules already cover. NorForge saw the campaign only because operators left their Cairn panels exposed to internet scanning.

Story 03

Gartner Maps 2027, and the Harness Is the Door

The map: at its IT Symposium, Gartner named its top ten strategic technology trends for 2027. Gene Alvarez, Distinguished VP Analyst, framed the problem bluntly: AI systems, he said, "operate in an architectural vacuum," producing software faster than people can review it and opening credential paths no one manages. Several trends are about agents directly. AI context platforms give agents the business knowledge they need to act, and Gartner predicts that organizations that prioritize semantics in AI-ready data will raise agentic accuracy by up to 80% and cut costs by up to 60% by 2027. The Agent Coding Harness is a closed-loop control system that governs coding agents across the software lifecycle. AI Agent Identity replaces static credentials with access granted per action.

The number that will get quoted: Gartner predicts that by 2028, 25% of enterprise security breaches will trace back to AI agent abuse, driven by unmanaged machine privileges and old credential practices. Story 02 is what that looks like at small scale.

My read: this is still evolving, and the harness is where it will be won. The harness is the agent's entry point to everything it touches, so it is where hardening happens: identity, permissions, checks before action. As harnesses mature, breach risk should shrink. Gartner's 25% figure assumes that gap is not closed. Mine is the optimistic case, and it depends on organizations actually investing in the harness rather than in more agents.

Where to start: Gartner's list reads like a build order. Give each agent its own identity (Agent 101, Issue #29: Non-Human Identity), put a harness around it (Issue #13: The Agent Harness), and feed it governed context. Skip the first two and the third makes a capable agent more dangerous, not less.

▌ Watch This

Gartner's 25% is a forecast of what happens if nothing changes. Budget for agent identity and the harness this planning cycle, and the forecast becomes a ceiling rather than a floor.

In simple terms

Gartner, a research firm many IT leaders follow, published its list of the technologies that matter most for 2027. Several are about controlling AI agents: giving each one its own login, putting a supervising layer around it, and feeding it accurate company information. Gartner expects a quarter of company security breaches to involve misused AI agents by 2028 if this is not done.

Under the Hood

Gartner defines an agent coding harness as a closed-loop control system: it constrains what coding agents can run, verifies their output, and feeds results back into the loop. Agent identity swaps long-lived static secrets for just-in-time, policy-controlled access, binding the model, prompt, and credential to each action so it can be audited.

Story 04

The Bill Is the Brake: Splunk's 55%

The survey: Cisco released the Splunk State of Observability 2026 report. Oxford Economics surveyed 2,050 IT operations and engineering professionals in 10 countries earlier this year. Note the source: Splunk sells the monitoring tools the report recommends, so read it as a vendor survey, not an analyst study. Its findings: 55% say they cannot control AI costs, and 60% say unexpected billing spikes strain them more than high baseline costs do. 57% say their telemetry costs now exceed the cost of the infrastructure being monitored. Splunk's "leaders" group is far more likely to attribute AI costs accurately to the agents and models that drive them (91% against 69%).

The line that stuck: Kamal Hathi, who runs Splunk at Cisco, said the excitement of AI "is clashing with the reality of the bill." That matches what the report measures: the problem is less the size of the bill than the surprise.

My read: unpredictable cost is now the brake. In the organizations I work with, it is the reason leaders give for pausing AI investment. A pilot that cannot say what it will cost next month does not get funded for next year, however good the demo was. Cost control has become the precondition for the next round of AI funding.

What follows: agents make this worse, because they loop, retry, and run in the background (Story 01). The tools that let you see cost per agent, per workflow, and per owner are no longer optional finance tooling. They are how a pilot earns its second year (see Agent 101, Issue #35: The Token Bill).

▌ The Lesson

Before the next pilot is approved, require that its cost can be traced to each agent and workflow, with a cap set. Predictable cost is now what gets an AI budget renewed.

In simple terms

A survey of more than 2,000 tech workers found that over half cannot keep their AI costs under control. Surprise bills are the biggest headache. When leaders cannot predict what AI will cost, they slow down or stop spending on it. The survey comes from a company that sells cost-tracking tools, so it has an interest in the answer.

Under the Hood

Attributing AI cost means tagging every model and tool call with the agent, workflow, and owner that triggered it, then rolling up tokens, retries, and calls per tag. Without that tagging, bills arrive per provider account, and a spike cannot be traced back to the loop that caused it.

Story 05

LiteLLM, Again: Vendors Can Sell the Hardening, Not the Accountability

What happened: LiteLLM, an open-source gateway that applications use to route calls to many AI model providers, was compromised for the second time this year. According to LiteLLM's own incident report, its release pipeline was hijacked and pushed version 1.107.0 to PyPI, the main Python package index, and to its official Docker images. The package carried malware that steals credentials and loads remote-control tools. It was live on PyPI for about two hours before being pulled. Datadog, citing LiteLLM's analysis, reports that the attackers used stolen GitHub tokens from three engineers' accounts. The earlier compromise this year hit two other versions, and no link between the two has been established.

Why this one matters more than most: a gateway like LiteLLM holds the API keys for every model provider an application uses. Datadog puts its downloads at about 23 million a week. Poison the gateway and you collect the keys to everything behind it.

My read: this forces a choice. Buy this kind of plumbing through a vendor, and the questions become what security control the vendor actually provides and how far its liability extends. Harden it yourself, and it becomes your own engineering problem. There is an opening for vendors to bundle the hardening with delivery and price the risk into the deal. But vendors can sell the hardening; they cannot take the accountability. Contracts usually cap liability at the fees paid, and a vendor's own pipeline can be poisoned too, exactly as LiteLLM's was. Whichever route you take, three baseline controls stay with you:

  • Pin and verify versions before you update, rather than taking whatever is newest.
  • Keep secrets out of logs, so a compromised host has less to steal.
  • Use short-lived, narrowly scoped keys, so one bad update cannot empty the vault.
▌ The Context

The AI supply chain is now a target in its own right. Whether you buy or build, the breach and the regulator still land on you, so own the three controls above.

In simple terms

Many AI apps use a free tool called LiteLLM to connect to AI services. Criminals broke into the system that publishes its updates and slipped in a version that steals passwords and keys. Anyone who installed that update during a two-hour window has to assume their systems were exposed. It is the second time this has happened to the same tool this year.

Under the Hood

The source tag for 1.107.0 was clean. The malicious files were added inside the compromised build environment, so every wheel uploaded to PyPI carried them. Open-ended constraints such as litellm>= and floating Docker tags such as latest pulled the release in automatically. Hash-pinned lockfiles and digest-pinned images would not have.

⚖ The Governance Angle

Five more signals from the week — where the rules, the contracts, and the limits around AI moved.

  • Know which model is inside your vendor's product: the US Department of Homeland Security ordered federal civilian agencies to remove DeepSeek products and stop buying them, and the order also covers third-party products built on DeepSeek implementations. It does not apply to private companies, but anyone selling to US civilian agencies now has to answer a model-provenance question (DeepSeek last appeared in Issue #38).
  • An AI diagnosis result, with the fine print: in a paper published in Science, Microsoft's MAI-DxO system diagnosed 83.2% of 304 difficult published case records, against 36.1% for 56 generalist physicians who could use reference materials. The authors are from Microsoft, the cases are a test set rather than live patients, and no specialists or physician teams were in the comparison, so this is evidence for a pilot, not for deployment.
  • Anthropic's pre-IPO governance move: Anthropic said it will commit 5% of its shares to a new Anthropic Public Benefit Fund, subject to stockholder approval, with the board able to add more as the company's value rises above an undisclosed baseline. Until the vote, treat it as a pledge rather than a transfer.
  • Sovereignty goes mainstream: Gartner predicts that by 2030 over 75% of organizations based outside the US will adopt digital sovereignty strategies, moving critical workloads to reduce geopolitical and vendor lock-in risk. For Canadian and European readers, exit plans from critical AI providers are becoming a planning item, not a theory.
  • A second Cairn group went after payment data: NorForge linked another set of exposed Cairn panels to an English-writing group that told its agents to steal credit cards and cryptocurrency. Agent-run attacks are now aimed squarely at payment data, which puts PCI scope and card-data controls back on the AI risk list (Story 02).
CIO Corner

From the editor's desk: Start With the Sponsor's Number

With Duet, Grok Bot, and Dots all heading toward the workplace, the questions I would ask from the CIO's seat are simple ones. Are we ready for the agent road? And where do we start: in operations as they run today, or by re-engineering the process first? The common pattern I see is scattered pilots, each with its own tool, and costs that are found late. The organizations getting it right are doing three things differently.

They start with a sponsor's measurable outcome. Not "try agents in finance," but a number a business sponsor already owns. Then they fix just enough of the process to make the agent's work measurable against it, rather than re-engineering everything before the first result.

They start where mistakes can be undone. Early agent work goes where errors are reversible, with a human approval on every step that is not (see Agent 101 below). And they settle on one agent platform rather than letting each team pick its own.

Put on the Radar: every agent pilot gets a spend cap and a named owner for its cost before it starts. If no one owns the bill, the bill will end the pilot.

▌ The Lesson

Tie each pilot to a sponsor's number, start where mistakes can be undone, and give every pilot a cap and a cost owner.

Agent 101

Reversibility

Think about writing in pencil versus pen. With a pencil you can try things, make mistakes, and erase them, so you can work fast without checking every word. With a pen, you slow down before you commit, because there is no eraser. Most people handle this without thinking: draft freely, then pause before the step that cannot be taken back, like signing a contract or dropping a letter in the mailbox.

Name and define: reversibility is whether an agent's action can be undone after it happens. A reversible action, such as drafting an email or moving a file to trash, can be rolled back. An irreversible one, such as sending a payment, deleting data permanently, or signing an agreement, cannot. Where an action cannot be literally undone, a compensating action can offset it: a refund reverses a charge, and a correction follows a sent message. Designing for reversibility means sorting every action an agent can take into one of these groups and treating each group differently.

Why it matters: this is how you decide where an agent may run on its own. Reversible steps can run unattended and be checked afterward. Irreversible steps need an approval gate before they run, which is exactly the line Claude Duet draws when it asks before it sends, pays, signs, or deletes (Story 01). It is related to Human-in-the-Loop Checkpoints (Issue #21) but comes first: reversibility tells you which steps need a human at all. It also tells you where to start a pilot (CIO Corner): wherever most of the work can be undone.

One technical line: tag each tool in the agent's interface as reversible, compensable, or irreversible; let reversible calls run with logging, pair compensable ones with a defined undo action (the saga pattern), and require a human-issued approval for irreversible calls, using idempotency keys so a retry never repeats a payment.

Let agents work in pencil. Make them stop and ask before they pick up the pen.

That's your signal for the week of October 18–24, 2026. Agents went to work for both sides this week: for the professional who wants an assistant, for the attacker who wants a cheap way in, and against the companies whose tooling they quietly pass through. The organizations that come out ahead will be the ones that decide who holds each agent's keys, what each one costs, and which of its actions can never be taken back.
